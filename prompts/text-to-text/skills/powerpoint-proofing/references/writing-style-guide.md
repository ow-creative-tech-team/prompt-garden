# Oliver Wyman writing style guide

## Core approach

- Written content is a visible expression of the brand: write with consistency, clarity, and precision.
- Use US spelling, grammar, and punctuation for global communications, marketing materials, and communications to all colleagues. Adapt to local style where content is exclusively for a local audience.
- For questions not covered here, use Merriam-Webster and the AP Stylebook for US English; Cambridge Dictionary/BBC News Style Guide for British English; ABC Style Guide for Australian English; and the Canadian Press Stylebook for Canadian English.

## Organization and people

### Company and businesses

- Refer to the enterprise as **Marsh**; its ticker is **MRSH**. The legal name remains **Marsh & McLennan Companies, Inc.** through at least 2026, and must be used in legal documents.
- After the first reference to Marsh, **the company** may be used once. Use lowercase for company, firm, organization, and businesses unless they are part of a formal name.
- During the 2026 transition: refer to the insurance broking business as **Marsh Risk**; use **Oliver Wyman, a Marsh business** externally; and describe Oliver Wyman Group as **Marsh Management Consulting** - never **MMC**.
- Do not describe practices or businesses as separate companies/legal entities. Use possessives: *Marsh's Casualty Practice*, *Mercer's Investment Consulting practice*.
- Capitalize formal practice/function/group names. Lowercase generic use and words such as team and function unless they form part of the formal name. At Marsh Risk, **Practice** is part of the formal practice name. **FINPRO** is always all caps, only for the FINPRO Practice; use *financial and professional* for generic products, risks, and issues.
- In external marketing, use lowercase functional descriptions rather than individual practice names where appropriate (for example, *Oliver Wyman's financial services solutions*).

### Colleagues and job titles

- In internal communications, use **colleagues**, not employees. Lowercase *colleagues* except in a proper name (for example, *Colleague Connect*).
- Capitalize a person's proper job title, but not level titles such as *partner*, *vice president*, and *managing partner*. Once introduced, refer to colleagues by first name, including Executive Committee members.
- Use **it/its** for a single entity, board, company, or group; use *they/their* only for the members of that group. Singular *they/them/their* is acceptable for a person when an alternative is awkward or when it is that person's stated pronoun; use a plural verb and *themselves*.

## Abbreviations and names

### General rules

- Spell out a term on first reference and add its abbreviation in parentheses; use the abbreviation after that. Do not define an abbreviation that will not be used again.
- Common abbreviations such as CEO, CFO, and FBI may appear on first reference. Abbreviations are also acceptable in titles/section headings. When in doubt, spell it out.
- Do not use periods in capital-letter abbreviations, including two-letter forms: **US**, **UK**, **EU**, **HR**, **IT**, **DC**, **DB**. Keep periods if they are part of a formal name (for example, U.S. Army).
- Use **Oliver Wyman** in all external communications; never use **OW** or another shortened form in marketing, publications, digital channels, press, recruiting, or client-facing content.
- Capitalize only proper nouns when spelling terms out: *third-party administrator (TPA)*, *interactive voice response system (IVR system)*.
- Form abbreviation plurals with a lowercase **s**, no apostrophe: *CEOs*, *OCIOs*. Use an apostrophe only for possession.
- Spell out US state names. **United States** and **United Kingdom** may be abbreviated to **US** and **UK**, as nouns or adjectives, even on first reference.
- Avoid Latin abbreviations in formal marketing/corporate prose. Prefer *for example* (with a comma), *such as* (no comma), or *that is* (with a comma). In constrained graphics, *e.g.* and *i.e.* take periods and a comma after the final period; *etc.* takes one final period.
- Refer to a law by its full name and acronym on first reference, then use the acronym. **the Act** or **the Directive** may be used on subsequent reference only when unambiguous.
- Use **Jr.** and **Sr.** only after a person's name and without a comma: *Joe Smith Jr.*

### Degrees and designations

- Capitalize formal degrees; lowercase general degree names and fields except proper nouns. Use *a Bachelor of Science in finance*, not *a Bachelor of Science degree in finance*.
- Spell out most degrees on first reference; MBA, MD, and PhD may appear on first reference. Do not use periods in degree/designation abbreviations.
- Offset a post-name academic abbreviation with commas: *John Jones, PhD, is ...*.
- Capitalize formal membership designations; lowercase generic ones. Use **CFA® charterholder** (lowercase) where applicable.

## Lists, capitalization, and titles

### Bulleted lists

- Use round bullets for primary lists and dashes for secondary lists.
- End introductory text with a colon, capitalize each first word, and aim for five items or fewer.
- Keep all items grammatically parallel. Use a period only for a complete-sentence bullet; use no end punctuation for fragments/short inventory items. Do not end a bullet with *and*, *or*, a semicolon, or a comma.
- For web copy, introduce every list and pair bullets with short paragraphs and clear subheads.

### Capitalization

- After a colon, capitalize only a proper noun or a complete sentence. In British English, capitalize only proper nouns after a colon.
- Lowercase *board* and *board of directors* unless part of a formal name; write **Marsh Board of Directors** in full.
- Use sentence case for headlines, headings, and graphic labels: capitalize only the first word, proper nouns, and acronyms.
- In body text, capitalize principal words in publication titles; lowercase articles and conjunctions, and prepositions of three letters or fewer. In British English, lowercase prepositions of any length.
- In headline-style hyphenated compounds, capitalize all words unless the compound is dictionary-recognized (for example, *Long-term Outlook*). In British English, capitalize only the first word of hyphenated compounds.
- Capitalize named regions; lowercase generic geographic references. Lowercase industries and sectors unless they contain a proper noun.
- Capitalize formal policy, form, and endorsement titles; lowercase generic insurance coverage, policies, forms, endorsements, and insured perils.

### Titles, citations, and sources

- Italicize the official titles of books, reports, magazines, newspapers, television programs/podcast series, and films. Do not italicize generic words not in the official title.
- Put article, essay, chapter, report-section, and television/podcast-episode titles in quotation marks. Do not quote generic sections such as *introduction* or *appendix*.
- Capitalize, but do not italicize or quote, campaigns, initiatives, websites, and software names.
- Prefer inline citations with public links for digital-first content. If third-party data is used in a chart, credit it in both body text and the chart.
- When notes are essential in long/print content, use endnotes, not footnotes. Put reference numbers outside punctuation except dashes.
- Endnote order: author, publication title, date, volume/issue, and page number(s). Italicize book titles, quote chapter/article titles, capitalize titles, use *p.* / *pp.*, and provide a full web citation (not only a URL). Use a DOI link for journal articles where available.

## Copyright and trademarks

- End external publications with the relevant two-sentence statement, for example: `Copyright 2026 Oliver Wyman LLC. All rights reserved.` Prefer the word *Copyright* to the © symbol.
- Where space is limited (PowerPoint, standalone graphics, ads), use `© 2026 Oliver Wyman LLC` with no punctuation; *All rights reserved.* may be omitted.
- Capitalize proprietary/trademarked names. Use ®, ™, or ℠ on first reference in every standalone asset; if it appears in a heading, also use the symbol on the first mention in body text. Use it occasionally in long content where it reinforces proprietary status. Put the symbol inside punctuation.

## Digital, web, and currency style

### Currency

- Do not capitalize or italicize currency units: *dollars*, *yen*, *euros*, *pounds sterling*.
- For global communications, use **A$**, **C$**, **HK$**, **S$**, **US$**, **€**, and **£**, with no spaces next to the amount: *US$25*, *£50 million*.
- Use a full currency name after the amount when helpful: *2,000 Canadian dollars*. Use *yuan*, not *renminbi*.
- When many currencies appear, use ISO codes after the amount: *2,000 USD*, *1 million EUR*, *500,000 GBP*.

### Web and digital content

- Use lowercase email and web addresses. Omit `http://`, `www.`, and other unnecessary subdomains; use the shortest useful URL.
- Hyphenate most *e-* words, but write **email** as one word. Use lowercase except at the start of a sentence or in a name that has its own capitalization.
- Use descriptive hyperlinks (content titles or relevant keywords), never *click here*. Avoid generic *learn more* links unless they are part of a larger campaign.
- On conversion/gated-content pages, use a specific call-to-value rather than a generic call-to-action. Do not link to other organizations' websites.
- Lowercase *web*, *internet*, and *intranet* unless they are trademarked/formal. Use one word for *website*, *webcam*, *webmaster*, and *webcast*; use two words for *web page*.
- Choose **webcast** or **webinar** and use it consistently within a communication/campaign.
- Use two words for *cyber insurance*, *cyber risk*, *cyber threats*, and *cyber exposures*. Most other *cyber-* terms are one word: *cyberattack*, *cybercrime*, *cybersecurity*, and so on.

## Dates, times, numbers, and percentages

### Dates and times

- Do not use ordinal dates. Spell out month names in running text; abbreviate months to three letters without periods only in tables/graphs.
- Use US global date style: *Month Day, Year* or *Month Day*. Insert a comma after the year in a complete running-text date. Use the local convention for exclusively local content.
- In running text, spell out *first quarter* through *fourth quarter*. **Q1 2026** is acceptable in charts/tables (or **Q1 26** when space is constrained; **Q1** alone only if the year is otherwise clear).
- Use the 12-hour clock for global communications, with time-zone abbreviations. Write *am* and *pm* with a preceding space; use *noon* and *midnight*; omit `:00` except to preserve parallelism.
- Use a no-space en dash for time ranges: *9 am–6 pm EST*, *1:30–2:30 pm PDT*. Use am/pm once only unless the range crosses noon/midnight. Avoid redundant wording such as *10 am this morning*.
- Approved time-zone abbreviations: **ET**, **EDT**, **EST**, **GMT**, **BST**, and **CET**.

### Numbers and percentages

- Spell out one through nine; use numerals for 10 and above. Spell out numbers at the start of a sentence except years, and preferably reword to avoid a percentage at the start.
- Use numerals with abbreviations, phases/sections, charts/tables, measurements, currency, time, and temperatures.
- When adjacent figures use different styles, distinguish them: *six 8-cent stamps*.
- Repeat the unit/symbol across a range for clarity: *5% to 7%*, *5%–7%*, and *$5 million to $7 million*. Do not shorten them to *5–7%* or *$5–$7 million*.
- Spell out *thousand*, *million*, *billion*, and *trillion* in prose; K/M/B/T are only acceptable in space-constrained charts/tables. Use commas in figures of four or more digits except in formal names that omit them.
- Use numerals for fractions greater than one and spell out fractions below one with hyphens. Prefix decimals below one with zero: *0.6%*.
- Use a numeral and **%** for numerical percentages in prose, charts, and graphics. Use *percentage* (not *percent*) for the noun.
- Distinguish a percentage change from percentage-point change; never state a decline greater than 100% or that a number became *fivefold lower*.

## Punctuation and formatting

### Apostrophes and commas

- Use apostrophes for possession, not plurals: *CEOs*, *URLs*, *the 1980s*. Use an apostrophe for plural single letters (*i's*, *t's*) and to shorten decades (*'60s*).
- Do not use possessive apostrophes in insurance-type descriptors, except **workers' compensation**.
- Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction only when both clauses are independent. Set off explanatory examples/appositives with commas.
- Use a comma after introductory phrases and transitions except *and* or *but*. Always use the serial (Oxford) comma in a list of three or more items.

### Dashes, ellipses, hyphens

- Never substitute a hyphen for an em dash or en dash.
- Use an **em dash** to set off an explanation or abrupt change in thought; use it sparingly and consistently. External publications typically put one space on each side.
- Use an **en dash** without spaces for ranges: *10–12 weeks*. Do not use an en dash after *from* or *between*; pair them with *to/through/until* and *and*, respectively.
- Use an ellipsis (`…`) with a space on each side, but no internal spaces, to show omitted words or hesitation. Add a period first if the preceding material is a complete sentence. Do not begin/end a direct quote with an ellipsis.
- Hyphenate compound adjectives except after an -ly adverb; do so even after a form of *to be*. Rephrase strings with too many hyphens.
- Do not hyphenate compound nouns unless the dictionary does. Hyphenate multiword prefix compounds, words where omission creates ambiguity, a prefix before a capital/numeral, and vowel-ending prefixes before the same vowel. Retain hyphens in status/occupation terms such as *co-author*.
- With **non-**, generally hyphenate only before another *n*; do not hyphenate if *not* can replace it. Use suspending hyphens in shared compounds: *long- and short-term*.

### Other punctuation and formatting

- Do not use periods after headings, headlines, or display text. Use periods for abbreviated words such as *Approx.*
- Use single quotation marks in headlines/headings and for a quote within a quote; use double quotation marks for ordinary quotations. Put periods and commas inside quotation marks.
- Use a comma before a full-sentence quotation but not a mid-sentence quote. Put a defined term in quotation marks on first mention.
- Use semicolons to distinguish complex elements in a sentence. Do not add spaces around a slash.
- Do not use **&** in place of *and* except in official names, accepted acronyms (for example, D&O and M&A on second reference), or approved cobranded-logo treatments.
- For emphasis, choose bold, italics, or color and use it consistently; do not underline text because it may look like a hyperlink.
- Use roman type for foreign words adopted into English and italics only for less common foreign terms. Do not italicize foreign proper names.
- Use one space after a sentence.
- Telephone format: country code, area/city code, and number separated by spaces, for example, `+1 212 345 7000`. Omit the country code only for a wholly local audience. Use **T** for office numbers and **M** for mobile numbers when needed.
